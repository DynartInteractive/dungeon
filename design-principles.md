# Design Principles — Lessons from Doom RPG

## Core Philosophy: Constraint Breeds Creativity
Every tile earns its place. Every room has a purpose. Every resource matters. Do more with less.

## 1. Every Tile Matters
- No filler corridors or empty rooms — every tile should serve a purpose (fight, secret, resource, lore, puzzle)
- Dense, compact level design over sprawling maps
- Floors should be small enough for the player to hold the mental map in their head
- Backtracking should feel intentional — "I remember where that locked door was"

## 2. Combat is Positioning
- Whether real-time or turn-based, spatial positioning should be the core tactical decision
- Seeing a room full of enemies should make the player stop and think: back up to funnel them? Use a side room? Burn a powerful consumable?
- Different enemies should have different movement behaviors — some fast, some slow, some charging — creating emergent tactics from simple rules
- Two-character party adds depth: who faces the enemy? Who stays back?

## 3. Resource Scarcity Creates Tension
- Don't shower the player with potions and ammo — make every resource decision matter
- Health stations / fountains at fixed locations — plan routes around them, don't hoard healing
- Weapon/spell resource costs should make the player think: "Is this enemy worth a fireball, or do I save it?"
- The feeling of being low on supplies in a dangerous area is the core tension loop

## 4. Weapon-Enemy Type Matchups
- Different enemies should be weak/resistant to different damage types
- The two characters should be able to specialize (one for fire enemies, one for undead, etc.)
- Using the wrong weapon should feel wasteful (more ammo spent, more damage taken)
- Discovering an enemy's weakness should feel rewarding

## 5. Pacing and Session Length
- Each dungeon floor should be completable in 10-15 minutes — ideal for mobile play sessions
- Pick up, play a floor, put down — respect the player's time
- Escalation should be gradual but relentless: introduce one new enemy type or mechanic at a time
- Each new element should change how the player approaches rooms

## 6. Environmental Personality
- Wall text, lore objects, readable terminals/books — cheap to implement, huge atmosphere payoff
- NPCs (even rare ones) break the isolation and add character
- Humor breaks tension — don't take it too seriously all the time
- Small memorable moments: a skeleton slumped in a corner with a note, a room that tells a story through item placement

## 7. Secrets in Plain Sight
- Slightly different wall textures, suspicious dead-ends, alcoves that hint at hidden passages
- The wall/column grid system is perfect for push-walls and hidden rooms
- Secrets should reward observation, not pixel-hunting — fair clues, satisfying discovery
- Secret areas should contain meaningful rewards, not just a health potion

## 8. Simplicity That Doesn't Feel Simple
- Strip mechanics to their essence — if a system doesn't add meaningful decisions, cut it
- Fewer but more impactful items over a bloated inventory of +1/+2/+3 variants
- Clear, readable enemy silhouettes — the player should know what they're facing instantly
- UI should be invisible: the player thinks about the dungeon, not the interface
